Interfaith wedding ceremony outline

How to include two traditions without making one the main event and the other a footnote.

Home › Ceremony Samples › Interfaith

Most interfaith ceremonies go wrong in the same way: one tradition gets the structure and the other gets a single reading near the end. Guests notice. The outline below alternates between the two, so neither side of the family spends the ceremony waiting for their part.

Order of the ceremony

  1. Processional, with music from both traditions
  2. Opening words
  3. Invocation or moment of silence
  4. Reading from Tradition A
  5. Reading from Tradition B
  6. Declaration of intent
  7. Vows
  8. Ring exchange
  9. Unity ritual
  10. Blessing from both traditions
  11. Pronouncement
  12. Recessional

Opening words

Officiant: We are here today for [Bride] and [Groom]. They come from different traditions and are keeping both. This ceremony was built by them, with both families in mind, and nothing in it was added by accident.

Write this down before you plan anything else: which tradition supplies the structure, and which supplies the content. Couples who skip this step usually end up arguing about details that don't matter.

Invocation

Neutral option: Let us take a moment of silence to acknowledge what we are here to do.

Dual option: We ask for blessing from the traditions of [Tradition A] and [Tradition B], and from everything this couple holds sacred.

Readings

Reading one, from [Tradition A]: read by a family member from that side.

Reading two, from [Tradition B]: read by a family member from that side.

If one family is larger or louder, give the smaller side the first reading. The room settles in the first two minutes. Whoever reads first sets the tone.

Declaration of intent

Officiant: [Groom], do you take [Bride] as your wife, honoring the traditions you each bring to this marriage?

Groom: I do.

Officiant: [Bride], do you take [Groom] as your husband, honoring the traditions you each bring to this marriage?

Bride: I do.

Vows

Interfaith couples usually write their own. If you are stuck, start with what you are promising about the other person's background, not about your own.

Groom: I, [Groom], take you, [Bride], as my wife. I promise to keep your faith and your family in our household, not as a guest but as a permanent part of it.

Bride: I, [Bride], take you, [Groom], as my husband. I promise to keep your faith and your family in our household, not as a guest but as a permanent part of it.

Ring exchange

Officiant: These rings are a circle. They belong to no single tradition. They mark what you are building together.

Unity ritual

Pick something that reads as neutral, or combine two small rituals. Sand blending, a shared cup, or two candles lit from one flame all work. Avoid any ritual that requires one side to perform something from the other side's tradition. It rarely lands the way couples expect.

Blessing

Dual option: May the God of [Tradition A] and the spirit of [Tradition B] bless this marriage.

Neutral option: May this marriage be steady, patient, and long.

Print a program. A one-page sheet that names each tradition and explains what each part is does more for family tension than anything said during the ceremony itself.

Pronouncement

Officiant: By the power vested in me by the State of [Your State], I now pronounce you husband and wife. You may kiss.

Recessional

Officiant: Ladies and gentlemen, Mr. and Mrs. [Last Name].
